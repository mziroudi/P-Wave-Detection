01 — Overview

The one question, asked sixty times a minute

A seismometer turns ground motion into a wiggly line over time — a seismogram. This project hands a small neural network a ten-second slice of that line and asks it one thing:

The questionDoes this slice contain the beginning of an earthquake, or is it only background noise?

The trick is to treat the seismogram like a short piece of audio: a fixed clip, sampled a hundred times a second, on three channels of motion — East, North, and Vertical. A 1-D convolutional network reads the clip and returns two probabilities: noise, or earthquake.

The repository goes further. A second model predicts exactly when the quake starts inside the clip. And the detector can run continuously over an hour of real data, or live against a streaming station. We will build up to all of it — and then take it apart.

02 — The science

Why a few seconds is everything

An earthquake radiates two body waves. The P-wave is a push–pull compression: fast (about 6 km/s) but faint — it arrives first, the gentle first tremor. The S-wave is a side-to-side shear: slower (about 3.5 km/s) but violent — it carries most of the damage, and it arrives later.

That gap between the harmless herald and the destructive follow-up is the whole game. Catch the P-wave the instant it lands, and you buy time before the S-wave hits. This is Earthquake Early Warning — detection in real time, not prediction of the future.

At 80 km from the epicenter, the P-wave arrives after 13.3 s and the S-wave after 22.9 s — a warning window of 9.5 seconds to act.

Notice how the window widens with distance: far sites get more warning, sites atop the epicenter get almost none — the "blind zone."

04 — The data

STEAD, and a portable slice of it

The STanford EArthquake Dataset holds roughly 1.2 million labeled three-component seismograms. Each trace is sixty seconds at 100 Hz — 6000 samples across 3 channels — and earthquake traces carry expert phase picks: the exact sample where each wave arrives.

To keep things runnable on one machine, the project pulls a public subsample from Zenodo. The waveforms arrive as HDF5:

KeyShapeMeaning
traces(N, 6000, 3)the waveforms — samples × E/N/Z
metadata(N,)names like AMT.HP_20120416124803_EV
p_arrival(N,)P-wave sample index
s_arrival(N,)S-wave sample index

Earthquakes live in test.hdf5; pure background lives in test_noise.hdf5. That the name string encodes both the station and the event time will matter later, when we hunt for leakage.

05 — Making windows

From a minute of signal to a ten-second lesson

The network wants fixed ten-second clips. The windowing code cuts them deliberately.

An earthquake window starts two seconds before the P pick, so it holds a little context and the first tremor. A random jitter of ±50 samples keeps the network from memorizing a fixed position. Exactly 1000 samples are taken, and the P's offset within the clip is recorded as the regression target.

A noise window is a random slice of a noise-only trace. A pre-P hard negative — a noise-labeled clip taken from before the P on an earthquake trace — is added to discourage firing on the quiet lead-in.

Finally, every window is z-scored per channel: subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation. This erases absolute amplitude, so a great quake and a small one look comparable.

Remember thisPer-window z-scoring, paired with the P sitting near a fixed index, is precisely what will let the model cheat. We return to it in section 11.
06 — The model

A modest stack of convolutions

A 1-D CNN slides small learnable filters along time, catching local shapes — a sharp onset, an oscillation — and composing them into richer features. Here is the exact path a clip takes; B is the batch size:

LayerShapePurpose
Input(B, 3, 1000)E/N/Z over 10 s
Conv 3→32, k7 · BN · ReLU · Pool(B, 32, 500)broad onset shapes
Conv 32→64, k5 · BN · ReLU · Pool(B, 64, 250)mid-scale patterns
Conv 64→128, k5 · BN · ReLU · Pool(B, 128, 125)finer features
Conv 128→128, k3 · BN · ReLU · AvgPool(B, 128, 1)summarize the clip
Flatten · Dropout · 128→64 · 64→2(B, 2)decide noise / earthquake

That is 110,466 parameters — deliberately small, because a real-time detector should be quick. The timing model, SeismicCNN1DRegressor, reuses this exact backbone and swaps the final layer for a single sigmoid unit scaled to the window length, emitting the P-arrival sample index.

07 — Training

Nudging the weights

Training adjusts those parameters until predictions match labels, judged by a loss and improved with an optimizer.

The data is split three ways: the model learns on train, we choose using validation, and we report on test — data it never saw. If test leaks into train, the test score becomes fiction. Hold that thought.

08 — Evaluation

The numbers, and how to read them

On the balanced test set the demo model reports 97.4% accuracy, a macro-F1 of 0.974, and a ROC-AUC of 0.994. The confusion matrix shows why it looks so clean:

Read those metrics precisely: precision punishes false alarms, recall punishes misses, F1 balances the two, and ROC-AUC is the chance the model ranks a random earthquake above a random noise clip. All of them, here, are measured on a tidy fifty-fifty test set.

09 — Timing

Not just whether, but when

Pinpointing the P-arrival inside the window matters for estimating warning time and magnitude. The regressor predicts a sample index; error is read in milliseconds. It reaches roughly 23 samples of mean absolute error — about 233 ms.

10 — Continuous & live

A sensor never stops

Continuous inference slides the ten-second window across a long recording, one hop per second, scoring every position. To avoid firing on every blip, an alert rule demands that the earthquake probability stay above a threshold (0.85) for several consecutive hops (3) — the same false-alarm control real early-warning systems use.

Before scoring, continuous data is detrended, tapered, band-passed between 1 and 20 Hz, mapped to E/N/Z, resampled to 100 Hz, and z-scored. The live loop does the same on the last ten seconds polled from a station each second. Whether this holds up on real data is exactly the right question — and the answer is uncomfortable.

11 — The reckoning

What did it actually learn?

Every earthquake example is quiet, then loud. A careful reader should ask: is the network learning P-wave physics, or an amplitude shortcut? The whole investigation reruns with one command, diagnose_shortcut.py. Here is the trail.

First, a myth

Maybe the flat lead-in is fake — literal zero-padding from trimming. Tested, and rejected: across 800 earthquake traces, none have an all-zero pre-P segment, and only about a tenth of a percent of pre-P samples are exactly zero. The lead-in is genuine ambient noise, roughly the same size as the noise-only traces.

Then, the real culprit

The earthquake coda is some twenty-seven times larger than its own pre-P noise. Per-window z-scoring divides everything by that large spread, crushing the real pre-P noise until it looks flat — while pure-noise windows stay uniformly loud. After normalization:

Normalized regionNoise windowEarthquake window
std of first 1.5 s0.990.23
std of last 6.5 s0.991.11

The proof

If the model rides the envelope, a trivial detector should rival it. It does — a single feature (the amplitude of the first 1.5 seconds) comes within two points of the network:

ModelAccuracyROC-AUC
Threshold on early-window amplitude95.4%0.979
Logistic regression, 5 envelope features95.3%0.991
SeismicCNN1D97.4%0.994

The reckoning on real data

On the ten quiet minutes before the Ridgecrest M7.1 even happens, the model calls the silence "earthquake" more than half the time — a mean probability of 0.54 and roughly 1,440 false alarms per hour at threshold 0.85. Its first "alert" fires about ten minutes before the quake exists.

And a quieter bug

Because the split is made at the window level with no grouping, about a quarter of test windows share their earthquake with a training window, and 94% share a station. The score is inflated by memorization.

The lessonBeautiful metrics can measure the wrong thing. Ask whether a trivial baseline could match you; whether the result holds on realistic, imbalanced, continuous data; whether the split is truly leak-free. Report false alarms per hour, not just balanced accuracy. The full write-up is here.
